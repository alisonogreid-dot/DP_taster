Next year you'll move from MYP into the Diploma Programme. The humanities subjects you can choose from sit in Group 3: Individuals and Societies. This site gives you a quick teaser of four of them — what you'd actually study, and one short skills task per subject so you can feel what the work is like. You've already done most of the underlying thinking in our MYP5 units — this is just naming it.

Group 3 · Individuals & Societies

History

First assessment 2028 · This is the new curriculum you'd take.

01What is DP History?

DP History is not about memorising dates. The IB describes it as a "dynamic, evidence-based, interpretative discipline" — a course that teaches you to "do history", not just study it. You inquire into the past through sources, build arguments, and engage with the fact that historians genuinely disagree about what happened and why.

Four things you'll do all the time

Inquire
Formulate your own historical questions and pursue them
Analyse Sources
Work with primary and secondary sources — read for content, context, and perspective
Build Arguments
Construct historical arguments using evidence; evaluate other historians' arguments
Compare Across Place & Time
It's a world history course — you'll study at least two regions

How it's structured

You'll choose one Focused Study (e.g. Climate and innovation, Conflict and displacement, Protest and change), one Thematic Study spanning 750 CE onwards (e.g. Authoritarian rule, Popular movements), and at HL, one Regional Study. You also run your own Historical Investigation — a self-directed inquiry on a topic you choose.

A taste of the content
Focused Study: Climate and Innovation

One of the new focused study options looks at how human societies have responded to environmental change and technological transformation over time. You might investigate, for example, how Norway's twentieth-century shift to oil-and-gas extraction reshaped Stavanger, what made earlier resource transitions succeed or fail (whaling to fishing, agriculture to industry), and how different societies have handled the political and economic consequences of innovation.

Sound familiar? The mode of thinking is exactly what you did in the Vanishing Points unit — analysing human-environment interaction across scales. DP History gives you the toolkit to do that thinking with academic precision.

From MYP5 to DP
You've already practised source analysis (Thunberg speech, Indigenous vs. Western forestry voices), historical inquiry (Easter Island narratives, the Aral Sea), and constructing arguments from evidence (Commons case studies). DP History formalises these and asks you to do them with denser sources and longer-form writing.

02Try It: Source Analysis

This task uses the structure of Paper 1, Question 1: explain how the content of a historical source can be used to answer an inquiry question. Real Paper 1 gives you two sources; we'll work with one to keep it short.

Inquiry question: To what extent did the discovery of North Sea oil transform Norwegian society in the 1970s?

Source A

"By 1975, scarcely six years after the Ekofisk discovery, the question facing Norway was no longer whether oil would come ashore but what kind of country oil would make. The Storting's Ten Oil Commandments of 1971 had insisted that petroleum activity must benefit the whole of Norwegian society — but the reality on the Stavanger coast told a more complicated story. Fishing villages found themselves transformed into helicopter bases. Wages in the platform yards pulled labour from inland farms. The Lutheran rhythms of the Rogaland coast bent uneasily around shift work on the rigs. Critics from the Centre Party warned that the country was becoming a 'petroleum colony of itself' — rich at the surface, hollowed underneath."

— Adapted from a historian's account of Norway's early oil years (secondary source, 2014).

Psychology

First assessment 2027 · A redesigned course (the older 2019 version is what current DP2 students sit).

01What is DP Psychology?

The course's goal is "psychological literacy" — not to make you a psychologist, but to give you the tools to think critically about claims about human behaviour. You'll get those claims everywhere for the rest of your life: in news headlines, social media, advertising, your own self-talk. DP Psychology trains you to ask: How do they know that? What's the evidence? What might be missing?

The new (2027) framework: Concepts, Content, Contexts

Concepts
Bias · Causality · Change · Measurement · Perspective · Responsibility — the lenses you apply to everything
Content
Three approaches: Biological, Cognitive, Sociocultural — plus research methodology
Contexts
Health and well-being · Human development · Human relationships · Learning and cognition
HL Extensions
Culture, motivation, technology in shaping behaviour — plus data analysis

Note on the older syllabus

Some of you may have heard about Psychology from older students. They study a different version (first assessment 2019): three approaches plus an options pathway (Abnormal, Developmental, Health, or Human Relationships). The redesigned 2027 course you'd take is more focused on thinking with concepts rather than memorising studies — closer to how MYP I&S has been training you.

A taste of the content
Cognitive bias and the news you read

One thing you'd study is how human thinking deviates predictably from rationality. Anchoring bias, for example: the first number you hear influences every subsequent judgement, even if that first number is irrelevant. Estate agents use it. Negotiation handbooks use it. Newsrooms use it when they lead with a casualty figure. You'd learn the mechanism, the research that demonstrates it, and the ethical questions about whether knowing about a bias actually protects you from it.

From MYP5 to DP
Your AI Stoplights work, the way we've talked about polemic as strategically one-sided rather than balanced, evaluating whose voice gets heard in the Indigenous-vs-Western forestry debate — all of that is concept-driven thinking about perspective and bias. DP Psychology gives those moves a research base.

02Try It: Critical Thinking on a Claim

This task uses the structure of a Paper 2 short-answer question: discussing a claim using the concepts. You're not expected to know psychological studies yet — just to think with the concepts.

A claim circulating online

— Style of claim you'd see in a news headline or social media post.

Global Politics

First assessment 2026 · The most contemporary of the four — you study current events as the syllabus.

01What is DP Global Politics?

Global Politics is the course for students who want to understand "how the world they live in works, and what makes it change (or prevents it from changing)." It's built around contemporary issues — so your textbook is, in a real sense, this week's news. The course draws on political science, international relations, and the social sciences to help you reason about power, conflict, rights, and development.

Four anchor concepts you'll keep returning to

Power
Hard, soft, structural, relational — who can make whom do what?
Sovereignty
Who has the right to make decisions over a territory or a people?
Legitimacy
Why do people accept (or refuse) a government's authority?
Interdependence
How states, organisations, and people rely on each other across borders

The three thematic studies

Alongside the core, you study Rights and Justice, Development and Sustainability, and Peace and Conflict. The course also requires an Engagement Project — you actually engage with a political issue (volunteering, attending public meetings, conducting interviews) and write about it.

A taste of the content
Climate justice and competing claims of sovereignty

Imagine a low-lying Pacific island state arguing at the UN that fossil-fuel-producing nations should pay reparations for sea-level rise. The legal case is complicated: states are sovereign over their own emissions policies, but the consequences cross borders. Who has standing? What counts as harm? What kind of power does a small state actually have against a global hydrocarbon economy? DP Global Politics gives you the vocabulary — structural power, soft power, international regimes, climate justice — to argue about cases like this with precision.

From MYP5 to DP
Your Vanishing Points work on resource equity, the Tuvaluan elder scenario, the Lagos student activist, the Sahelian farmer — these are all live Global Politics cases. The polemic-vs-balance distinction, the questions about whose voices count: all DP Global Politics moves.

02Try It: Identifying Actors and Power

This is a warm-up version of what DP Global Politics asks all the time: given a real-world scenario, identify the actors involved and the kinds of power at play.

Scenario

In 2023, a coalition of small Pacific island states submitted a resolution to the UN General Assembly requesting that the International Court of Justice issue an advisory opinion on the obligations of states to protect the climate system. The resolution was co-sponsored by 132 countries and was passed by consensus. Major fossil-fuel-producing nations did not formally oppose the resolution but expressed concern about its scope in private bilateral discussions. Greenpeace and the Pacific Islands Students Fighting Climate Change (PISFCC) network had spent four years lobbying for the resolution.

— Based on the March 2023 UNGA resolution A/77/L.58.

Economics

First assessment 2022 · The most technical of the four — but built around the same nine concepts throughout.

01What is DP Economics?

Economics starts from a single problem: scarcity. We don't have unlimited resources. Choices must be made. The course studies how those choices get made — by individuals, by firms, by governments — and what consequences follow. It's analytical and uses diagrams and some maths, but at HL there's a strong policy element where you reason about how governments should act.

The nine key concepts (running through every unit)

Foundations
Scarcity · Choice · Efficiency
Values
Equity · Economic well-being · Sustainability
Dynamics
Change · Interdependence · Intervention

What you actually study

Four units: Introduction to economics, Microeconomics (how individual markets work — supply, demand, why markets sometimes fail), Macroeconomics (the whole economy — growth, unemployment, inflation, monetary and fiscal policy), and The Global Economy (trade, exchange rates, development). Internal assessment is a portfolio of three short commentaries on real news articles.

A taste of the content
Inflation, trust, and Norway's oil fund

You'd revisit content you've already touched on in the Money unit, but with sharper tools. Why does a central bank raise interest rates? (To dampen aggregate demand — a Keynesian move.) Why did Norway create a sovereign wealth fund rather than spending oil revenue immediately? (Sustainability, interdependence with future generations, avoiding "Dutch disease" — the way a resource boom can distort an economy.) DP Economics gives you the diagrams and vocabulary to explain all of this precisely.

From MYP5 to DP
Your Money unit covered scarcity, monetary policy, inflation/deflation case studies, the 2008 financial crisis, Bretton Woods, the Triffin Dilemma, and behavioural economics. You've also touched the Quantity Theory of Money. DP Economics formalises all of that with diagrams (supply and demand curves, AD/AS models) and asks you to apply it to news articles every couple of weeks.

02Try It: Apply a Concept to a Real-World Snippet

This is the structure of the IA portfolio: you read a short news extract and explain it using economic concepts. You don't need diagrams yet — just clear reasoning.

News snippet (adapted)

"Norges Bank, Norway's central bank, raised its key policy rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4.5% on Thursday — the highest level in over fifteen years. Bank governor Ida Wolden Bache said the move was necessary to bring inflation, currently at 4.8%, back toward the bank's 2% target. Mortgage holders face higher monthly payments; exporters welcome the slightly weaker krone the rate decision is expected to produce. Critics from the Centre Party warned that the rate rise will hit rural households disproportionately."

— Style of an Investopedia / news commentary item, illustrative.
